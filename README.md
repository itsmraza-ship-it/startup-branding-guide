## 1. Choosing a Company Name

Your name is the hardest-working asset you will ever own. It appears in every search result, invoice, contract, and introduction — and changing it later means re-filing, re-printing, and re-teaching every customer who already knows you. Spend real time here.

### What makes a name strong

- **Distinctive.** It stands out from competitors and is capable of trademark protection. Invented words, unexpected combinations, and suggestive names are far stronger than generic descriptions.
- **Sayable and spellable.** If someone hears it once — on a call, a podcast, a referral — can they type it correctly into Google? If not, you will pay for that friction forever.
- **Short.** Two to three syllables, ideally. One or two words. Short names fit logos, handles, domains, and memory.
- **Expandable.** It should not trap you. A name tied to one product, one city, or one service line becomes a cage when you grow.
- **Right-feeling.** It should sound like the business you intend to become — premium, playful, technical, trustworthy — to the specific people you serve.

### Checks before committing (do all of them, in this order)

Do not print anything, incorporate, or announce until every check passes. Use the full step-by-step version in [naming-checklist.md](naming-checklist.md).

1. **State business-name availability.** Search your formation state's business entity database (Secretary of State). Your exact legal name must be available — and remember, state approval is *not* trademark clearance. The state will happily register a name that infringes someone else's trademark.
2. **USPTO trademark search.** Search the US Patent and Trademark Office database (TESS / Trademark Search) for your name *and* close variants, in your industry and adjacent ones. Look at live marks first. A confusingly similar mark in a related field is a stop sign, not a suggestion.
3. **`.com` domain availability.** Check whether `yourname.com` is available to register at a sane price. If the exact `.com` is taken by an active business, that is usually a reason to pick a different name — customers will type `.com` no matter what your real extension is. Alternatives (`.co`, `.io`, country domains) work for some niches, but decide consciously, not by default.
4. **Social handle availability.** Check the exact handle (or a close, consistent variant) on the platforms you will actually use — Instagram, TikTok, X, LinkedIn, YouTube, Facebook. One consistent handle everywhere is worth real money in recall.
5. **No confusing similarity to competitors.** Google your name. Search it in app stores, marketplaces, and your industry's directories. If a competitor's name is one letter, one sound, or one word away, customers *will* confuse you — and reviews, payments, and legal letters will go to the wrong company.
6. **Say-it and spell-it test.** Say the name to five people once, out loud. Ask them to write it down and to find you online. Note every misspelling — that is your real-world data.
7. **Language and meaning check.** If you sell internationally, check the name (and its common misspellings) in your customers' languages for unintended meanings.

### Common pitfalls

- **Geographic names.** "Karachi Consulting" or "Texas Tea Co." limits expansion, dates quickly if you move, and is hard to trademark. Exceptions exist (location *is* the brand) — but make it a choice, not an accident.
- **Purely descriptive names.** "Fast Cheap LLC Filing LLC" describes instead of distinguishes. Descriptive names are weak trademarks, blend into every search result, and sound interchangeable with ten competitors.
- **Hard spellings and clever substitutions.** Dropped vowels, swapped letters (`Lyft`-style respellings only work with a marketing budget), numbers, hyphens, and homophones ("rite" vs. "right") create permanent friction in domains, handles, and word of mouth.
- **Initials and acronyms.** Meaningless to a new customer, impossible to search, and almost always already taken as a domain and handle.
- **Trend-chasing.** Suffixes and formats that are fashionable this year (`.ai` clones, "-ify", "get-") will look dated fast and make you indistinguishable from this year's cohort.
- **Naming for today.** Naming the company after your first product, first client, or a founder in-joke you will have to explain for a decade.
- **Skipping the trademark check because the state said yes.** The single most expensive naming mistake. State registration ≠ the right to use the name commercially.

### Worked example — "Epics Deals"

- Distinctive two-word combination — not a description of LLC filing, so it can stretch across formation, tax, compliance, media, and tech.
- Sayable and spellable on first hearing, in English and for non-native speakers (its core market is non-resident founders).
- Domain and brand built around `epicsdeals.com`; name, domain, and handles align.
- Deliberately *not* geographic and *not* service-locked — the company serves founders in any US state from anywhere in the world.

## 2. Picking a Niche

A brand cannot be built for "everyone." Positioning starts with choosing exactly who the brand is for — and, just as importantly, who it is not for.

### Start with a narrow wedge

Pick the smallest segment you can serve exceptionally well and win outright, then expand from a position of strength.

- **Who, precisely?** Not "small businesses" — "non-resident founders forming their first US LLC."
- **What painful, specific problem?** Not "business services" — "forming, banking, and staying compliant in the US without a US address, SSN, or local knowledge."
- **Why you, credibly?** A lived experience, a price point, a speed, a language, a partnership network — something a generalist cannot copy by next week.
- **Can they pay, and can you reach them?** A perfect niche you cannot find on any channel, or that cannot pay your price, is a hobby, not a wedge.

Widen only after the wedge works: same customer, adjacent problem — then adjacent customer, same problem.

### The positioning statement formula

Fill this in. If you cannot fill it in without hand-waving, your niche is not chosen yet.

> **For** [specific customer] **who** [specific problem / situation], **[Brand] is a** [category] **that** [single key benefit / outcome]. **Unlike** [main alternative / competitor], **we** [clear differentiator].

**Worked example — Epics Deals:**

> For non-resident and US-resident founders who need to start and run a US company without an address, SSN, or local tax knowledge, **Epics Deals** is a US business formation and compliance company that handles the LLC, EIN, banking guidance, and ongoing tax filing end-to-end. Unlike DIY filing portals, we pair the filing with partner banking access, a founder community, and a compliance team that files before every deadline.

Notice what the statement does: names the customer, the situation, the category, the outcome, the alternative, and the difference — in two sentences. Everything in the brand kit below exists to make that statement feel true on sight.

## 3. Building a Brand Kit

A brand kit is the small, fixed set of decisions that makes everything you publish recognizably *yours*. Keep it to one document (use [brand-kit-template.md](brand-kit-template.md)) and actually enforce it.

### 3.1 Logo

- **Primary logo** — full lockup (mark + wordmark) for website headers, documents, and presentations.
- **Secondary / stacked version** — for square and centered layouts.
- **Icon / mark only** — for favicons, avatars, app icons, and watermarks.
- **Clear space** — define a minimum empty margin around the logo (e.g., the height of one letter) that nothing may enter.
- **Minimum size** — the smallest the logo may appear before the icon-only version must be used.
- **Don'ts** — no recoloring, stretching, rotating, drop shadows, outlines, or placing the logo on clashing backgrounds. Ever — including by partners and contractors — unless the kit explicitly allows it.

Export and store: SVG (source), PNG with transparent background (light and dark versions), and JPG on solid background. Name files predictably: `logo-primary-dark.svg`, not `final_FINAL_v2.png`.

### 3.2 Color palette

Three tiers are enough for almost every startup:

| Tier | Job | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | The color people remember you by. Headers, key buttons, brand moments. | One color. Use it consistently and sparingly enough that it still means something. |
| **Accent** | Highlights, calls to action, charts, secondary emphasis. | Must contrast with the primary and with your neutrals. |
| **Neutrals** | Backgrounds, body text, borders, surfaces. | A near-black, a mid-gray, and an off-white cover almost everything. |

Record every color three ways: **HEX** (web/design), **RGB** (screens), **CMYK** (print) — and check text/background combinations for contrast (WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for body text).

**Worked example — Epics Deals palette:**

| Color | HEX | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Epics Yellow | `#FED716` | Primary — energy, optimism, the highlight color in every creative |
| Epics Black | `#0A0A0A` | Neutral / base — backgrounds, text, premium contrast against the yellow |

A single electric primary against a near-black neutral is the whole system: yellow-on-black for headlines and CTAs, black-on-white/yellow for body contexts. Two colors, zero ambiguity — anyone on the team can produce on-brand work without asking.

### 3.3 Typography

Two typefaces. That is the kit.

- **Headline font** — carries the personality. Used for titles, hero text, and social creatives.
- **Body font** — carries the readability. Used for paragraphs, documents, captions, and UI.

For each, record: font name, weights you actually use (e.g., 400 / 600 / 800), where it is used, and a **fallback** (what to use when the font is unavailable — e.g., in email or a partner's PowerPoint).

**Worked example — Epics Deals typography:**

| Use | Font | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Headlines | **Sora** | Bold, modern, geometric — titles, hero copy, creative headlines |
| Body | **Inter** | Clean, highly legible at small sizes — paragraphs, documents, web body text |

### 3.4 Voice and tone

Voice is *how your brand sounds when it talks* — fixed. Tone is how that voice flexes by situation — a launch post and an apology do not sound the same, but both should sound like you.

Write down:

- **Three voice adjectives** (and what each one rules out).
- **Do / don't examples** — real sentences, rewritten. This is worth more than any adjective list.
- **Vocabulary** — words you use, words you never use, how you name your products/services and your customers.
- **Formatting habits** — capitalization of the brand name, emoji policy, how prices and numbers are written.

**Worked example — Epics Deals voice:**

- **Direct and warm.** Say it plainly, say it like a person, get to the point — then be generous with help.
- Taglines (record yours exactly, and where each is used):
  - Main tagline: **"Global Business Expansion, Made Easy"**
  - Partner line: **"Your Partner in Global Expansion"**
- Do: short sentences, real numbers, the customer's outcome first.
- Don't: hype claims you cannot prove, legal jargon where plain words work, pressure or fake urgency.

### 3.5 Usage rules

The kit only works if it answers, in advance:

- Who may use the brand assets, and where they get them (one canonical folder/link — not "ask in chat").
- Logo/color/font rules for partners, affiliates, and press — and what needs approval.
- Template-first rule: social posts, proposals, invoices, and decks are made *from templates*, not from scratch.
- The legal name vs. brand name rule: when the full legal name (with entity suffix) is required — contracts, filings, invoices — and when the short brand name is used everywhere else.

### 3.6 Asset checklist

Your kit is complete when all of these exist in one place:

- [ ] Logo files (primary, stacked, icon — light and dark, SVG + PNG)
- [ ] Favicon and social avatars generated from the icon
- [ ] Color palette with HEX / RGB / CMYK values
- [ ] Fonts (files or links) + fallbacks documented
- [ ] Tagline(s) recorded verbatim
- [ ] Voice do/don't examples (at least 5 of each)
- [ ] Templates: social post, presentation, proposal/document, invoice, email signature
- [ ] Photo / illustration style notes (what imagery fits, what never does)

Fill yours in with [brand-kit-template.md](brand-kit-template.md).

## 4. Consistency Rules Across Socials, Website, and Documents

A brand is repetition. People trust what they recognize, and they recognize what they have seen *the same way* many times. These rules protect that repetition:

1. **One name, one handle, one avatar everywhere.** Same company name spelling, same (or closest possible) handle, same logo avatar on every platform. Never a photo, a different logo variant, or last year's design on one forgotten profile.
2. **Same palette and fonts on every surface.** Website, Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn, YouTube thumbnails, PDFs, invoices — if a customer screenshot any two, they should match without labels.
3. **Same tagline, word for word.** Taglines are quoted exactly as recorded in the kit. No paraphrasing, no "improving" it per post.
4. **Templates, not improvisation.** Every recurring format (quote card, announcement, offer post, document) has a template. New format? Make it a template first, then post.
5. **Voice check before publish.** Does this caption/email/document sound like the do examples or the don't examples in the kit? One read-through, every time.
6. **Profile hygiene on a schedule.** Quarterly: audit every profile — bio, link, avatar, banner, pinned post — against the current kit. Platforms accumulate stale banners and dead links silently.
7. **One source of truth.** The brand kit document is canonical. If a file disagrees with the kit, the file is wrong. Update the kit first, then re-export assets from it — never the reverse.
8. **Partners use the kit too.** Anyone posting on your behalf — agency, affiliate, co-marketing partner — gets the kit and the usage rules *before* they get the logo files.
9. **Change the kit deliberately.** Rebrands and refreshes happen in the kit document, with a date and a rollout across all surfaces in the same week — not as a slow drift where the website changes in March and the TikTok avatar changes never.
10. **Legal and formal documents are not exempt.** Proposals, agreements, and invoices carry the same logo, palette, and typography. Trust is built (or lost) fastest on the documents attached to money.

### Consistency audit (5 minutes, quarterly)

- [ ] Google your company name — do the top results look like one company?
- [ ] Open every social profile side by side — same avatar, same name, current tagline/bio?
- [ ] Download your own latest proposal/invoice — does it match the website today?
- [ ] Check three recent posts on three platforms — same colors, same headline font, same voice?
- [ ] Fix every mismatch this week; update the kit if the kit was the thing that was wrong.

## Putting It Together

1. Vet and lock the name — [naming-checklist.md](naming-checklist.md)
2. Write the positioning statement (Section 2) — one page, before any design work
3. Fill in the brand kit — [brand-kit-template.md](brand-kit-template.md)
4. Build templates from the kit, then publish *only* from templates
5. Audit quarterly (Section 4)

Branding is not a logo. It is a name you cleared properly, a niche you chose deliberately, and a small set of decisions you repeat until the market repeats them back to you.
